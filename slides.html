Knowing How Without Knowing What

  • Typists hit about 6 keys a second, yet can’t say which finger presses which key.
  • Our question: how can we perform a skill without being able to describe what we know?
  • Quick demo: write the QWERTY letters on a blank keyboard, from memory.

Key terms: two kinds of knowing

  • Explicit knowledge: we can recall it and put it into words.
  • Implicit knowledge: it shows up in performance, not in description.
  • Automaticity: a practised skill runs without attention.
  • Hierarchical control: an outer loop picks the words, an inner loop picks keys and fingers.

Where this fits in our course

  • Implicit vs explicit memory: different systems, different ways to test them.
  • Amnesia: patients can learn skills without remembering the practice.
  • Forgetting: was it lost, hard to retrieve, or never encoded?
  • Skill stages: novices think it through, experts just do it.
Presenter 1

Study 1: Thinking about your hands slows them down

Logan & Crump (2009). Psychological Science, 20(10), 1296–1300.

  • 16 touch typists typed words after a cue: whole word, or only left- or right-hand letters.
  • Hand cues slowed typing by about 47%. Colour cues caused no slowdown.
  • Continuous text: about 80 wpm normally, about 14 wpm for one-hand letters.

The typing system uses hand–letter knowledge but can’t report it.

Presenter 2

Study 2: Typists can’t fill in a blank keyboard

Snyder, Ashitaka, Shimada, Ulrich & Logan (2014). Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics, 76(1), 162–171.

  • 100 typists (about 72 wpm) placed only 57% of letters correctly, yet typed at about 94% accuracy.
  • Even a recognition test reached only about 85%.
  • 24 typists learned Dvorak for about 1.5 hours: their recall was no better than for QWERTY.

Authors’ reading: explicit key knowledge may never be learned.

Presenter 3

Study 3: Same result, even for messy typists

Logan, Ulrich & Lindsey (2016). J. Exp. Psychology: Human Perception & Performance, 42(12), 2084–2102.

  • 48 typists, standard vs nonstandard finger use. Only 10 of the 24 “standard” typists really were.
  • Both groups recalled about 15–17 of 26 letters, but typed about 98% of letters correctly.
  • Hand cues slowed both groups by about 440 ms.
  • Nonstandard typists relied more on vision.

Why is it still unanswered?

  • The studies show explicit knowledge is poor, not why: forgotten, never encoded, or hard to reach?
  • “Never learned” comes from skilled QWERTY typists learning Dvorak, not true novices.
  • Test format matters: mimicking typing raised cued recall (about 79% vs 67%).
  • Every study is a snapshot. None follows one learner over time.

Our experiment: watch the knowledge form

  • Question: as people learn to touch-type, does explicit key knowledge rise then fade, or never form?
  • Who: about 20 hunt-and-peck typists learning touch typing over 5 short sessions.
  • Each session: typing speed and accuracy, plus key recall in two formats: pen and paper vs “air typing” with hands on the keyboard.
  • Measures: letters placed correctly (of 26), wpm, accuracy.

What each result would tell us

  • Recall rises, then falls as speed grows: knowledge fades with automaticity.
  • Recall stays low while speed climbs: never encoded.
  • Air-typing recall beats paper recall: part of the “gap” is the test.
  • Control: counterbalanced test order; session 1 is each person’s baseline.

Limits, and the takeaway

  • Small sample and 5 sessions can’t settle the debate.
  • Weeks of practice are not years; volunteers are self-selected.
  • Poor recall alone doesn’t prove implicit knowledge. We need converging tests.

Skill can run ahead of what we can explain.
